## Context

The ceremony this change rewrites already has the shape it needs: one dial on a dedicated protocol, a one-time secret verified and burned before any state, a commit that precedes the reply, uniform refusals, and a rollback that leaves the dialing node clean. What it does not have is any reason for the newcomer to believe the identity it is joining is the one on the screen. `PdnId` is 32 random bytes minted by `create` with no key material behind it, so the payload's identity is an assertion, not a commitment, and the tickets in the reply lead wherever the answering device wants them to lead.

KERI closes that gap with mechanisms doing different jobs, and attributing the work to the wrong one is how this design goes wrong. Cooperative delegation binds a device identifier to the identity's root identifier through a pair of events — the delegate names its delegator, the delegator seals the delegate — and a validator must find that seal in the delegator's log before accepting the delegated event. That chain makes an identifier meaningful; on its own it says nothing about who is on the other end of the socket, because anyone who has seen a log can replay it. The anchoring event authorizes this delegation and is durable and reusable by design, so it does not prove who answers now. A linked newcomer therefore signs a request transcript binding its device identifier to the exact authenticated `NodeId`, and the inviter signs a manifest covering the same endpoints, identifiers, and a fresh nonce. The founding device has no linking ceremony, so creation produces the other tagged `DeviceBindingProof` variant: the device signs its endpoint and the root countersigns the same binding. Every device record can therefore be classified from replicated proof. What binds bootstrap tickets to the identity is different again: the identity's log seals role-tagged digest commitments to exactly 2 device-replicated stores (D15), so a reply offering another store is refused before import without publishing namespace capabilities in the log.

The whitepaper (Smith, "KERI Design" v2.63, sections 7.25 and 9.5) is the conceptual source; it is not the wire specification, and this change does not follow it literally — see D1. Three constraints from our own code shape everything below: a ceremony frame is capped at 65,536 bytes, the fork's ingest hook sees a signed entry and never the bytes behind it and never a local write, and a device's iroh endpoint key already serves the transport's own authentication.

A fourth constraint decides the shape of this change and is stated first because everything about key material depends on it: **the runtime keeps nothing across a process.** `SyncNode` documents itself as in-memory storage, its blobs are a memory store, its documents are a memory instance, and the endpoint is bound without a saved secret key, so the node id is new in every process. What the existing tests call a restart is a fresh node re-importing a ticket from a live peer. Every durable thing this change needs — keys, the attempt, the pinned heads, the key events, the mark that an inception is unfinished — has no floor to stand on until a storage change puts one there. D14 therefore makes that storage change a hard predecessor rather than an optional sequencing choice.

## Goals / Non-Goals

**Goals:**

- A device refuses to join an identity whose history does not reach the identifier its payload names, before it imports anything.
- Every device of an identity can invite another, with no quorum, no shared private keys, and no root authority on the inviting device.
- A linking attempt interrupted by the network or by a restart converges on one device identifier and one anchoring seal, not on one of each per attempt.
- What the newcomer accepted as an identity's history is durable and monotone: a later replay of a different branch is refused rather than believed.
- Every property ADR-0012 bought stays bought: one dial, one bidirectional stream, one exchange, verify-and-burn before state, commit before the reply, uniform refusals, no reconciliation in the critical path, and a dialing side that after a failure holds no replica, no binding, and no hosted identity — while deliberately keeping the attempt, the pinned heads, and the ceremony context.

**Non-Goals:**

- A direct-mode validator receipt, witnesses and indirect-mode receipts, and their accountability thresholds. The first release replays the history and pins it locally; it is deliberately not called direct mode, since that mode's validator returns a signed receipt and becomes an implicit witness, which this change does not do. Duplicity between a controller and the rest of the world is not detectable here, and no requirement may imply it is.
- Verification of a counterparty's devices. The device sets published into connection metadata stores stay as they are; replacing them with verifiable material is the next change.
- Key rotation, revocation, device removal, and the "active chain" policy for the descendants of a removed device.
- Resolving a device identifier to a current transport endpoint after the ceremony, migrating or superseding a confirmed endpoint binding, and founder endpoint recovery. D9 distinguishes that general endpoint operation from the immutable replicated proof required here. A linked device may later forget and re-link from another live inviter; a founder cannot safely use that path because forgetting also removes the root keys. Re-incepting a founder under the root is recovery/supersession and is intentionally not hidden inside this change.
- Persistence itself, which is the precondition described in D14 rather than a part of this change.
- `did:keri` in UWill, and any change to the byte form of `PdnId`.
- Implementing the existing `PdnOp::{AddDevice, RevokeDevice, RotateKey, ActiveDevices}` operations. They remain reserved wire/domain variants, are not emitted or accepted by the KERI identity service, and cannot be interpreted as this change's create/link/forget operations; a later domain-API change must replace or version them before use.

## Decisions

### D1. The wire profile is delegator-field-only, and the whitepaper is not it

A delegated inception carries the delegator's identifier in a `di` field; the location of the delegating event is fixed by the seal in the delegator's log alone. The whitepaper's figure 12.7 puts a delegating location seal — prefix, sequence number, ilk, prior digest — inside the delegated event itself, which forces the delegate to know where the delegator's future event will sit before it can compute its own identifier.

Chosen because that difference decides three things at once: with the location inside the event, the ceremony needs either two exchanges or a prediction that any competing local event invalidates, and the device identifier depends on the anchor's location, so a retry after a dropped connection yields a different identifier and idempotency becomes impossible. Delegator-field-only keeps one exchange, keeps the identifier independent of the anchor, and matches the implementations we can test against.

The cost is honest, and it is more than a citation mismatch. Section 7.23 and figure 7.14 define an event-location seal as prefix, sequence number, ilk, and prior digest and state that those 4 values together uniquely identify an event location; section 9.5.2 then applies that seal to delegation. Without the location, nothing in the delegated event fixes where its seal sits, so the same inception can be sealed by the same delegator twice at different sequence numbers. What this change gets is a local property — the inviter does not seal an inception it has already sealed — not a global one.

This is no longer left to an implementation task. `pdn-node-keri-wire-profile` normatively fixes KERI 1.0 compact JSON field order, version-size encoding, BLAKE3-256 and Ed25519 CESR codes, thresholds, witnesses, exact signature bytes, canonical application transcripts, store-commitment preimages, and a complete root/founder/anchor vector. `keripy` `v1.0.0` at commit `6478fee8688a0a3e978827d5327f8fd05e3d3737` reproduced that vector and is the pinned compatibility oracle; the written bytes remain authoritative.

### D2. An identity is a root identifier; a device is a delegate of the device that invited it

The graph shape is a product decision, not a consequence of KERI, and it cannot be deferred: a delegate's delegator is fixed at inception and never changes, so an error here is repaired only by re-incepting every device.

Two coherent shapes exist. Flat — every device delegated by the root — gives one place to look and one authority that can act on any device, but the root's signing keys must then be on whichever device invites, which means copying them everywhere and losing the property that makes delegation worth having. A tree — each device delegated by its inviter — needs no shared keys and lets any device invite, which is what the existing non-founder-invite scenario requires.

The tree is chosen, with its consequences stated rather than smoothed over: authority over a device belongs to its own delegator and to no one else, so this change gives an identity no administrator — a claim about what the runtime provides, not about what the mechanism permits, and the difference matters, because the whitepaper's nested delegation recovery (section 11.6.1) lets a superseding rotation at a higher level recover keys signed or pre-rotated at any lower level, and says so of arbitrarily deep chains: "this cascades the security of the key management infrastructure of higher levels to lower levels". In a tree of devices that reads as an ancestor holding recovery authority over its whole subtree. None of it is implemented here — recovery, rotation, and revocation are all out of scope — so the honest statement is that this change offers no path to it, not that the shape forecloses it, and the topology question in front of product is therefore not only "who may invite" but "who may later recover whom"; losing a delegator freezes establishment for its subtree, and the way out is a new inception under a live ancestor, which means a device identifier is not a permanent name in the domain, so no claim, grant, capability token, or connection record is keyed by it — while its own key events, endpoint statements, and ceremony state are, being the record of that identifier and nothing else; and "a removed ancestor removes its descendants" is not KERI semantics but an application policy that a later change has to write.

### D3. The device keeps its own pre-rotated keys, so recovery is joint and revocation is absent, and root keys are only durable

The alternative is custodial pre-rotation, where the delegator holds the delegate's next keys and can rotate the delegate without it. That is not key sharing — the keys are the delegator's from the start — so the choice is about where rotation authority sits, not about hygiene.

Ordinary rotation authority sits with the device: a delegated rotation is signed by the delegate's pre-rotated keys and anchored by its delegator, and both are required, which is the whitepaper's joint-compromise property — "any exploiter that merely compromises only the delegate's authoritative keys may not thereby capture the control authority of the delegate. A successful exploiter must also compromise the delegator's authoritative keys" (section 7.25). This runtime implements no rotation or recovery, so exposure or loss leads to a new device identifier here. The mechanism is wider: section 11.6.1 permits a higher-level superseding rotation to recover signing or pre-rotated keys at lower levels recursively. A non-root device may therefore be recoverable by a future ancestor-recovery implementation even when its own keys are lost; the root has no such ancestor.

What this does not buy is a general "nobody can act on a device without it". Nested delegation recovery (D2) gives an ancestor a superseding path that this change neither implements nor forbids. This profile also claims no KERI accountability for what compromised keys signed: it has neither witnesses nor the validator-signed receipt that makes a direct-mode validator an ersatz witness. Its narrower local rule is that once this runtime pins an event it will not silently rewrite that accepted prefix; recovery may restore future authority, but the local pin remains until explicit forget. Any statement about accountability thresholds is deferred to the later receipt/direct-mode profile.

Revocation is out of scope for a second reason that outlives this decision: it would not take back the write ticket a device already holds. That is a capability question, and it belongs with UWill.

The root's keys follow from the same shape, and an earlier draft of this design overstated them. In a tree the root delegates only the first device, so losing the root keys does not stop an identity from linking devices: what it forecloses is rotating the root, recovering the first device, and — once the store set is sealed in the root's log (D15) — ever adding a store to that set. All three are outside this change, and the third is the one that turns "losing the root keys" from a foreclosed future into a ceiling on the identity's shape. Root keys are therefore required to be durable — that is what keeps those futures open — and an exported recovery copy is not required here, because nothing in this release would read it. The decision about such an export, and about where it may live, belongs with the change that first needs it.

### D4. Message, event, and bundle sizes are distinct bounds

A full replay of the root and every ancestor has no upper bound, and a frame is capped at 65,536 bytes, so the reply cannot be one message — not "does not fit today" but "cannot be made to fit in general".

The reply is frames on the same bidirectional stream. The wire profile owns both byte limits: 65,536 for each complete canonical `PDL1` body, excluding its 4-byte length prefix, and 61,440 for signed-event material — exact event JSON plus its fixed 88-byte indexed signature. That shared event limit also governs request inceptions, stored `KEL1`, and durable reconstruction sets. A stored event cannot be wider than a streamed one: every accepted event must remain replayable to another device under the same profile. Profile 1 defines manual request/event/manifest/tickets/end bodies instead of making Serde/postcard layout a protocol. Reply-bundle bytes sum every complete reply body, including the explicit end, and exclude only prefixes; the request is separate. The flat manifest inventory makes the event ceiling derivative as well as political: its body is `572 + 33 * N`, so 1,968 events produce 65,516 bytes and 1,969 produce 65,549, over the frame limit. The selected profile limits are therefore 1,968 event frames per reply bundle, 16,777,216 encoded reply-body bytes, 32 delegation edges from root to newcomer, and 1,024 accepted events per identifier.

Delegation depth is checked as depth-plus-one when an invite is minted, not as depth: a device exactly at 32 would otherwise print invites that always fail at redemption, after the secret is already burned. Events per identifier is a product ceiling in disguise — verifying a seal needs the delegator's log up to it, so the bundle carries every ancestor's events, and the bound therefore caps how many devices one device can ever invite over its lifetime, and how many times it can supersede anything else it records in its own log. At 1,024 it stops being able to invite; a later verifiable-checkpoint profile is the designed escape. Minting reserves 1 delegation edge, 1 inviter event, the maximum permitted anchor frame contribution, and the anchor's 33-byte inventory growth; under the log lock the inviter sizes the exact prospective manifest before writing the anchor. One absolute caller deadline is computed before dial and every later phase consumes only its remainder; no phase resets or owns a fixed slice.

The request carries one field the earlier draft did not have: a nonce the newcomer generates for this exchange, which the inviter's manifest signature covers (D5). It costs 32 bytes and no round trip — the request is already the first message — and it is what turns the inviter's signature into an answer to a challenge rather than a statement it could have made before the connection existed. Without it the ceremony's only fresh material is the newcomer's inception, which a retry deliberately reuses.

Tickets travel with the proof, and a newcomer whose verification fails discards them without importing. After it verifies the reply and consumes the verified ticket pair, it signs confirmation locally; that creates a signed local-completion boundary without another network round trip and without holding tickets back. It is not a KERI receipt and does not make the ticket bytes or capability verdict independently reproducible by another device.

### D5. Three canonical transcripts become a linked binding proof

The newcomer signs the profile-1 request transcript. The inviter signs the profile-1 manifest over the shared bindings plus anchor, signing-key state, and exact frame inventory. After verifying those objects, the chain, store commitments, and ticket capabilities, the newcomer signs the profile-1 confirmation over the request and manifest signed-object digests, its endpoint, both commitments, anchor, and newcomer key state. `pdn-node-keri-wire-profile` fixes every byte and domain label; no implementation-selected serializer is part of the contract.

The first 2 signatures prove the offered binding and the inviter's live response. They do not prove that the response arrived or was accepted: every directory writer can read a pending offer and copy bytes. The third signature is the newcomer-only transition from offer to confirmed proof and covers the facts verified after the reply. It requires no new message.

The inviter's signature is the ceremony's proof of live control. It is made under the keys the verified chain establishes for the chain's last identifier, and it covers a nonce that did not exist before this exchange, so it cannot be replayed from a captured session and cannot be produced by anyone who merely holds a copy of the identity's logs. This is the load-bearing signature, and an earlier draft of this design put the load on the anchoring event instead. That was wrong for a reason worth keeping in view: a retry deliberately reuses the anchor, so an anchor may be hours old and says nothing about who is answering now.

What the manifest half of that signature buys is narrower than it looks. A third party cannot reorder or cleanly truncate a QUIC stream, so the manifest is not a defence against the network. It binds the inviter to the exact inventory it chose to send and makes a stream missing an inventoried frame detectable. It cannot prove that this inventory was the inviter's complete history: an unpinned newcomer cannot detect valid events the inviter omitted from both stream and manifest.

The domain label keeps a signature made here from being presented as one made in another ceremony; both endpoints keep a captured request from being replayed on a second connection inside the secret's lifetime; the nonce keeps the inviter's answer tied to this exchange.

`DeviceBindingProof` is a tagged union with 2 closed variants. `Linked` contains all 3 canonical signed objects and anchor location. Pending instead contains `PendingLinkedOffer` with only the exact request and manifest; it is sufficient for uniqueness and retry, never for access. The newcomer durably adds confirmation after verification and writes the completed proof to confirmed. `Created` exists only for the founder and contains the profile-1 founder statement signed by the device and countersigned by the root. A missing applicable signature or fields reconstructed from local trust are insufficient; copying a proof to another path fails because every variant binds the exact subject `NodeId`.

### D6. The critical section takes the log under a write lock

Order: verify and burn, check the inception and canonical request, take the log's write lock, reject conflicting pending or confirmed bindings, build the prospective anchor and snapshot, check the bounds, write exactly that anchor, sign the manifest, construct canonical `PDR1` around the `PendingLinkedOffer`, commit its verified observation/supersession intent, write the pending record, mint tickets, and stream the same proof bytes. The completed `DeviceBindingProof` does not yet exist; only the newcomer creates it after verification.

The lock is not caution. Without one span around lookup and pending write, 2 endpoints holding the same device keys can both observe absence and obtain valid offers. Another event can also land between snapshot and anchor and invalidate the manifest. Under the lock the uniqueness decision, snapshot, anchor, manifest, and pending offer stand or fall together.

"Nothing irreversible has happened yet" is true only of durable state: the invite is burned by then, so a limit failure at redemption refuses uniformly and leaves no key event and no directory entry, but it does not leave the invite reusable.

### D7. Authority, discovery, and access stay separate, and the pinned head outlives the attempt

The anchor is append-only: a reply lost after it leaves a permanent record of a device that never came up. The anchor alone grants signing authority, not access, and a directory record alone must not grant identity authority. Current `access.rs` grants full access from confirmed alone, while any directory writer can create that record. This change keeps only discovery record-level. `pdn-node` owns KERI validation and publishes a complete generation-numbered `IdentityAccessProjection` into `data-layer`; `AccessBook` consumes that projection and never interprets KERI itself. `SessionAccess::Full` is a cached joined verdict over authenticated `NodeId`, accepted anchored chain, and a confirmed proof with every applicable signature: request, manifest, and newcomer confirmation for `Linked`; founder statement and root countersignature for `Created`. Pending carries no confirmation and grants nothing. A complete proof copied to another path names the wrong endpoint. Independently valid grant access remains possible.

Projection replacement is a security transition, not eventual cache refresh. The projection names the exact directory-device-input generation, proof digests, and `{aid, sequence, SAID}` head snapshot behind its monotonically increasing projection generation and replaces the whole identity-device decision set atomically. Any relevant record or payload-availability change advances the input generation, synchronously closes the old projection, and notifies the runtime before the new bytes are evaluated. Hosting starts closed. An identity-device `Full` decision becomes visible only after its KERI state is durable and the matching projection is installed. A transition that can remove `Full` first places the identity's device-derived access in a closed generation, invalidates deposited write admissions, and terminates active sync sessions authorized by the older projection; only then may the new KERI verdict be published. A cross-backend intent makes crashes and reopen remain closed until catalog state and `AccessBook` agree. A detected or not-yet-evaluated conflicting candidate therefore cannot leave an old `Full` session or write admission alive; grant-derived access remains independent.

A device identifier cannot move to another endpoint in this change. The standalone storage predecessor preserves the endpoint key, so an honest retry uses the same node id; another node id means a copied device key or corrupted clone and is refused while either a pending or confirmed binding exists. If replication nevertheless exposes valid confirmed proofs for one device identifier at 2 node ids, no arrival order or author id chooses a winner: every conflicting binding loses full access and the runtime reports the conflict until a later explicit endpoint-supersession operation resolves it. A pending record still grants nothing, cleanup tombstones it after 24 hours, and a fresh invite from the same endpoint converges.

The pinned head is the opposite kind of state. If a failed import removed it, an inviter could present a different branch on the retry and have it accepted as first-seen. It is therefore written after verification, kept with the durable attempt, continued by any retry, and removed only by an explicit act of forgetting an identity. "A failed link leaves no residue" means no replicas, no bindings, and no hosted entry — not that nothing at all remains.

### D8. Key events live in the directory replica, under content-addressed keys, with the accepted branch in a local view

The fork's ingest hook takes a signed entry and the sending peer; it never sees the payload bytes, and it is never consulted for a local write. Signature checking, digest chaining, and delegation are therefore not enforceable there, and this change does not ask the fork for anything.

Entries are keyed by identifier, sequence number, and an event-content digest: BLAKE3-256 over the exact final serialized event bytes. That storage digest is not the event's `d` SAID, which is independently verified through the profile's size-correct dummy serialization. Keyed without the content digest, two versions of one event from one author collapse by last-writer-wins and the loser disappears, and versions from different authors survive only until a query folds them — which makes the outcome depend on how the reader asks. Content addressing keeps every version as its own entry, and the decision moves to where it belongs.

The digest covers the event and not the signatures attached to it, so content addressing alone does not make the payload a function of the key. This profile closes the gap rather than leaving it: one signing key, a signing threshold of one, a single-key pre-rotation commitment, and Ed25519, whose signatures are deterministic — so one author's payload for one event is one fixed byte string, and a rewrite is byte-identical rather than a competing version under the same key. A profile with several signers, or with partial attachment sets arriving separately, breaks that and needs either the attachment set inside the address or a deterministic merge of attachments; both are out of scope here, and the constraint that keeps them out is written into the profile instead of being assumed.

The accepted branch lives in a durable runtime-owned local view built when event bytes arrive either in a verified linking reply or from the directory, 1 pinned head per identifier. Each head retains the exact canonical `KEL1` reconstruction set and delegation-seal index needed to re-verify it without waiting for replica payloads; the head and evidence are one security record. A ceremony snapshots `{aid, sequence, SAID}` for every head it accepted. Its recorded generations are conditional-write guards, not foreign keys that must continue to equal the current head generation. When a head advances, its new reconstruction set retains the old exact bytes as a prefix; restart validates an older ceremony by locating each snapshotted event and requiring a byte-identical verified prefix through it. A later head may extend that prefix, while a missing or different event at the snapshotted position fails closed. A delegated inception or rotation is not admitted merely because its own signature and chaining verify: it remains awaiting delegation until the accepted branch of its fixed delegator contains the matching seal, and is re-evaluated when that branch advances. A seal on a competing delegator branch authorizes nothing. `data-layer-kel-store` owns only the disjoint entry layout, canonical payload integrity, enumeration of every candidate, and payload-arrival notifications; `pdn-node-keri-identity` is the single normative owner of accepted-head, awaiting-delegation, gap, and discrepancy semantics. It is local by construction: replicas have no global order, and asking pdn-store for one would be asking the wrong component. A competing version stays in the store and is surfaced as a discrepancy.

Key events stay inside the directory replica rather than in a replica of their own, so an identity keeps exactly one device-internal replica and "the directory synced but the other store silently did not" stays unrepresentable.

### D9. The ceremony binding is required here; general endpoint resolution remains later

Session classification is already a reader of a durable device-identifier-to-endpoint binding. A record path plus an accepted KERI chain cannot supply it: a directory writer can copy legitimate bytes, and record authorship is an unrelated key on every import. The binding required here is therefore `DeviceBindingProof` from D5. Request and manifest bind both endpoints and live inviter control; pending replicates those 2 offer signatures. Only after verification does the newcomer sign confirmation and create the complete 3-signature `Linked` proof in confirmed. At session time the cached projection verifies the proof, path, authenticated `NodeId`, chain, and anchor together. A directory writer can neither move the proof to another node id nor promote pending without the newcomer signature.

This proof is immutable ceremony evidence, not a resolver. It says which endpoint completed creation or linking and cannot be updated after confirmation here. A request for the same device identifier from another node id is refused even while pending, and several confirmed bindings fail closed. A linked device can be forgotten and linked as a new identifier from another live inviter. A founder has no safe equivalent: forgetting removes its root keys, and using the root to establish a replacement founder endpoint is a recovery/supersession operation deferred to the later endpoint change. A sole founder that loses its confirmed endpoint key therefore cannot regain full access from another node id in this profile. Making the KERI key double as the iroh endpoint key remains rejected as cross-protocol reuse.

### D10. `PdnId` keeps its 32-byte form

The domain identifier appears in hundreds of places and inside store keys — `connections/<peer>`, `tickets/connection-metadata/<peer>/own`, `retractions/<issuer>/…` — and the code already separates it from the KERI type. With the derivation code and digest algorithm pinned by the profile, 32 bytes map canonically to a qualified identifier and back, so the qualified form lives at the KERI boundary and the domain type does not move. Turning the whole model into a qualified string is a separate decision with a migration behind it.

### D11. Durable boundaries, written down as a table rather than left to each site

Every step below either is durable or is not, and the answer decides what a restart does. The specs carry the requirements; this table is what they are read against.

Creating an identity:

Sealing the identity-store commitments (D15) reorders this: both replicas exist before the root is incepted, because their role-tagged commitments enter the inception configuration. The existing `SyncNode::create_namespace(issuer: PdnId)` cannot express that order because the issuer does not exist yet. Creation therefore uses a 2-phase data-layer API: prepare the directory and data replica without an issuer binding and return durable opaque handles plus their role-tagged `StoreCommitment` values; record those handles and commitments; derive the root; then bind the prepared data replica to the resulting `PdnId`. No namespace identifier crosses above `data-layer`, and no unrecorded prepared replica is adopted during recovery.

| Boundary | Durable after it | A restart here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outer creation intent written with every preallocated key handle and type | a non-admissible owner record naming all future secrets, but no key bytes yet | resume its recorded secret creates or roll back every handle; no registered key can be orphaned |
| Every root/device key registered; active creation record committed | root and device keys reachable from exactly that record | resume from the record; no identifier exists yet |
| Directory and data replicas prepared, their opaque handles and role-tagged commitments written into the creation record | 2 unbound replicas and the record naming them | resume with those exact prepared replicas; a replica created but never recorded is unreferenced and left to be dropped |
| Inception configuration fixed, root and device inceptions derived | the identity's identifiers, deterministic in the record | resume; the same identifiers come out, because everything the digest covers is in the record |
| Root inception written into the directory | the first event of the root log | resume by deriving the same device inception and root interaction event |
| Founding device inception written, still awaiting delegation | the first event of the device log, with no accepted device authority yet | resume by writing the deterministic root interaction event that seals it; no confirmed device record or Full access exists in this state |
| Root interaction event carrying the founding-device seal written | the accepted root-to-device delegation | resume by generating the same creation binding proof; rewriting either content-addressed event is idempotent |
| Prepared data replica bound to the derived `PdnId`; founding `Created` proof recorded as confirmed; data ticket published | the 2-store identity set and a verifiable founder endpoint binding | resume each idempotent binding/write; no marker-only founder record is permitted |
| Identity hosted, creation record cleared | a complete identity | nothing to do |

Startup recovery owns every unfinished creation record and completes all of them before the identity service accepts calls. It does not turn the next `create()` into a retry: the public call has no idempotency key, several records may be unfinished, and no evidence correlates one of them with that caller. The recovered identities appear through `hosted_identities`; every subsequently admitted `create()` starts another identity. Preserving caller-level retry semantics would require changing the API and is not claimed here.

Linking, dialing side:

| Boundary | Durable after it | A restart here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outer attempt intent written with preallocated key handles, inviter, identity, and local `NodeId` | a non-admissible owner for every future attempt key | complete all key registrations and activate the exact attempt, or delete every owned handle |
| Active attempt record committed before the dial | keys, assembled inception, identity, inviting device identifier, local `NodeId` | a retry reuses it only against the same inviter and from the same local endpoint; a mismatch abandons the attempt before dialing and assembles a fresh inception |
| Reply verified | pinned heads with exact `KEL1` reconstruction sets and seal indexes, exact signed request and manifest, anchor, snapshotted `{aid, sequence, SAID}` heads, and their commit-time generations | the attempt continues against the pinned branch and exact offered endpoint binding; later head extensions are accepted only when the snapshot remains a byte-identical prefix, while a different branch is refused even if ticket verification later fails |
| Both tickets verified | no new durable state; one process-local opaque byte-bound `VerifiedBootstrapPair` | a live attempt can only consume that pair; a restart re-dials and re-verifies against the already pinned reply evidence rather than reconstructing a handle from verdicts |
| Verified pair's consuming import intent and protected pair-secret record written; directory imported and armed | protected exact tickets behind an opaque handle, their digests, roles, identity, fallback, recovery owner, first replica, and classification binding | data-layer recovery validates and uses only that protected pair or rolls it back — a replica with no hosted entry is not left standing |
| Same consuming import completes the data namespace and final catalog state | exact directory/data replica identifiers, roles, hosted identity and issuer binding from the verified pair | recovery no longer needs ticket bytes, deletes the protected pair record through generic secret lifecycle, and rollback restores a binding this import displaced; the opaque pair cannot be reused |
| Own inception written; confirmation transcript signed and ceremony record completed | the newcomer's inception and complete 3-signature `Linked` proof | resume with the same canonical confirmation bytes; the inviter's anchor is never re-authored here |
| Confirmed device record written | the device in the confirmed set with the same complete `DeviceBindingProof`; the linking commit point has passed | rollback is disarmed; the identity and its imports are retained, and only the pending tombstone remains as resumable cleanup |
| Pending device record tombstoned | the device only in the confirmed set | cleanup is complete; repeating the tombstone is idempotent |
| Attempt closed | a hosted identity | nothing to do |

Linking, inviting side:

| Boundary | Durable after it | A restart here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Secret burned | nothing — pending invites are in memory | the invite is gone entirely, which is indistinguishable from never having existed |
| Anchor written under the log's lock | the seal, permanently | a repeat of the same inception finds the seal and does not write a second |
| Pending device record written | canonical `PDR1` containing creation time and signed `PendingLinkedOffer`, expiring after 24 hours and granting no access | only a verified offer receives cataloged first-observed state; rewrite cannot refresh it, supersession replays through its intent to exactly one active digest, another node id is refused, and no writer can complete it without the newcomer key |
| Reply streamed | nothing new locally | whether it arrived is unknowable here, which is why the newcomer confirms itself |

The device record's key and the ceremony's subject are not the same thing, and the retry path is where that shows. A record lives at `devices/<node-id>`; the identifier being registered is the newcomer's device identifier. D14 preserves the endpoint key, so a normal retry presents the same inception from the same authenticated endpoint and is idempotent. The durable attempt records that local node id. If an explicit profile migration or reset gives the runtime another node id before completion, it discards that attempt's inception, device keys, and endpoint-specific local proof before dialing and assembles a new inception; any pinned identity heads remain. The old inviter-side pending proof grants nothing and expires normally. Presenting the old inception from the new endpoint is never attempted and would be refused. The uniqueness lookup and pending write sit under the inviting device's log lock so 2 simultaneous endpoints cannot both observe absence. If conflicting confirmed proofs still arrive through replication, every conflicting endpoint fails closed.

### D12. Pairing is left as it is, and that is now a choice

Both ADR-0011 and ADR-0012 mark the same step deferred, and this change closes it on one of them. After it lands, a linking payload's identity is a cryptographic commitment while a pairing invite's identity is still a bare assertion: a peer can claim any `PdnId` in the pairing dialogue and nothing contradicts it.

That asymmetry is deliberate and bounded by what the two ceremonies hand over — a whole-directory write ticket against per-connection read tickets — and by scope: proving control in pairing needs the counterparty's history, which needs the verification of a counterparty's devices that this change explicitly excludes. It is recorded here so that it reads as a decision rather than an oversight, and so the next change inherits it as work rather than as a discovery. Two artefacts keep the old wording and are left alone on purpose: the pairing spec's own "bearer-free" heading and scenario, and the deferred-KERI sentence in its purpose.

### D13. The receipt, when it comes, is a KERI receipt

This change sends no receipt (D4). When a later profile adds either the direct-mode validator receipt or indirect-mode witness receipts, they SHALL be standard KERI receipts in the protocol's own format, stored where receipts belong — not the application-level acknowledgement or newcomer confirmation renamed. Calling either of those a receipt would claim accountability semantics without the signed KERI receipt that establishes them.

### D14. Durable storage is a hard precondition, and it is a change of its own

Nothing in this design invents its own persistence. The active `durable-runtime-storage` change comes first: documents and blobs on disk, protected endpoint/runtime-author/generic secret records, durable prepared-replica handles, successor-owned runtime-security records, atomic conditional catalog batches, write-ahead intents, and restart tests that reopen the same directory. It knows no KERI event or ceremony semantics. This change registers the `keri-v1` owner and assigns meaning to four opaque families: accepted heads, ceremony records, verified pending observations, and KEL problems. Root/device keys use the secret facility and pre-identifier replicas use prepared handles. The predecessor SHALL be validated, implemented, and landed before any implementation task here begins.

Two consequences follow. The durable requirements — pinned heads and problems surviving restart, atomic head/ceremony acceptance, verified first-observed state, crash-consistent pending supersession, an idempotent same-endpoint retry, and startup recovery of unfinished creation records — are testable rather than aspirational. The immutable ceremony binding cannot agree with "the endpoint the ceremony authenticated" if the honest device gets a new endpoint key on every start, and a `devices/<node-id>` record goes stale on every start for the same reason; the storage change removes both defects before this protocol exists.

There is no in-process fallback inside this change. Removing the durable requirements would change its security posture and recovery semantics and therefore requires a different proposal, not an implementation-time interpretation of this one.

### D15. The identity's log seals commitments to its 2 device-replicated stores, because the chain otherwise proves the wrong thing

A chain of seals proves who is answering. It says nothing about where the tickets in the same reply lead, and without a rule about that, this change would buy an identity's identifier and hand over its content to whoever answers.

The gap is in the code as it stands and is not hypothetical: the dialing side pushes the inviter's address into the directory ticket and imports it, then calls `import_namespace` with the payload's identity and whatever data ticket the reply carried, so the issuer binding is made from an unverified field. The directory replica itself keeps no owner — its documentation says the holder knows which identity the handle serves — so nothing downstream can catch the mismatch either. An inviter that genuinely controls a device identifier under identity X can therefore answer with a chain that verifies perfectly and tickets to identity Y's stores, or to a pair of fresh replicas it forked for the occasion, and the newcomer will host them as X. Every check in the ceremony passes; the device ends up writing its claims into a store the identity does not own, and reading a device set the attacker composes. A runtime hosting several identities makes this cheap, because it holds real tickets to more than one store set already.

The fix is to make exactly the 2 device-replicated identity stores part of what the identity commits to: the private-metadata directory and data namespace. A raw namespace identifier is itself a read capability, so the KEL SHALL NOT publish it. Profile 1 fixes `StoreCommitment` as BLAKE3-256 over `ASCII("mee-pdn.store-commitment.v1") || 0x00 || role-octet || 32 raw namespace bytes`, with role `0x01` for directory and `0x02` for data, encoded as CESR `E` and sealed in that order. `data-layer` alone computes and jointly checks both. It always evaluates the complete pair and returns its 2 verdicts in directory-then-data order on failure; the runtime maps every non-match into the correspondingly ordered public failure list. Only 2 internal `MatchWritable` results produce a non-cloneable `VerifiedBootstrapPair` that owns the exact canonical bytes, roles, commitments, and authenticated fallback address. Its consuming recoverable import is the only bootstrap import API. During replay the exact pair lives in an owner-only generic secret record referenced by a non-secret intent; after final catalog state names the imported replicas, that temporary secret is deleted. The runtime and general catalog therefore see neither namespace identifier nor decoded ticket, and the runtime cannot check one byte string and import another. `data-layer-identity-store-bootstrap` owns the prepare/bind and verified-pair contracts so they survive archival as data-layer requirements.

The term identity store set means only those 2 device-replicated stores. Pairing's connection metadata replicas are per-connection stores, reached through tickets carried by the already verified directory, and are deliberately not sealed into the root or counted as extensions. Cross-identity data replicas reached through grants are also outside this ownership claim: their issuer binding is authorized by the grant path and does not claim that they are stores of the hosted identity. The 2 commitments are fixed in this change; changing them or adding another identity-owned role needs a further root event. The commitment establishes ownership, not capability level, so each bootstrap ticket must also carry write authority.

The alternative was to check the reply's data ticket against the copy published in the directory under the `data` kind, which needs no new event. It was rejected because it can only run after the directory has caught up, which is after the imports — turning a refusal before any state into a rollback after 2, and leaving the directory itself, the more valuable of the 2, unchecked. The directory is the replica that has to be verified before import, since every per-connection store is discovered from it.

### D16. The empty claim proof is removed rather than filled with unrelated ceremony evidence

`PdnIdentityProof {}` serializes an empty object and can prove nothing, while the `Claim` model gives it a name that invites callers to treat it as evidence. The binding proofs introduced here establish hosted-device membership and endpoint possession; they do not sign a claim's attribute and capability, and placing one in `Claim` would conflate those statements. This change therefore removes the empty type and field, keeps `issued_by` as non-authorizing attribution, and rejects the former serialized shape. There is no deployed claim-proof format to migrate. The counterparty-verification change must introduce a new versioned proof only after it defines the claim bytes, signer key state, and verification failures.

## Threat model

Written out because the specs are read against it, and because a reader who sees "KERI" will otherwise credit the ceremony with more than it does.

| Threat | What answers it | What remains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The answering device does not control the identity it names | the chain of seals establishes that the identifier belongs to the identity, and the inviter's signature over the manifest — made under the keys that chain establishes, covering the newcomer's nonce and both endpoints — establishes that the party answering holds those keys now. The anchoring event authorizes the delegation and is deliberately reusable across retries, so it carries none of this load | nothing, once the profile and its verification are right |
| The answer is replayed from a session someone recorded | the nonce the newcomer generated for this exchange, inside the signature | nothing, given a nonce from the operating system's generator |
| The chain verifies but the tickets lead somewhere else — another identity of the same inviter, or a forked pair of replicas | the 2 role-tagged store commitments sealed in the identity's inception (D15), compared inside `data-layer` against both tickets before either is imported | commitments hide the namespace read capabilities but do not prove ticket capability level; write authority is checked separately |
| The dialogue is relayed or the request replayed elsewhere | the request transcript, covering both endpoints of this connection under a domain label | nothing |
| The payload is substituted whole, before the human sees the genuine one | the out-of-band channel the human used — a screen in the same room — plus the secret's two-minute life | not closed by this change and not closable inside it; a second factor out of band is the only lever |
| The payload is photographed | the secret's lifetime and single use | whoever presents it first within that window links; the payload carries nothing long-lived |
| An observer of the payload learns which identity is being joined | nothing | accepted, as it is for pairing's invite; per-context identifiers are what KERI makes cheap later, and they are not this change |
| The inviter sends a stream inconsistent with its manifest, or denies later what it inventoried | the signed manifest binds the inviter to the exact frames it lists, so a missing, extra, reordered, or changed inventoried frame is detectable | an inviter may omit valid history from both stream and inventory; without a prior pin or witnesses the newcomer cannot know that omitted events exist |
| A hostile inviter streams without bound | the five bounds, enforced while reading rather than after | a slow honest inviter and a hostile one are still distinguished only by the caller's budget |
| The identity's own devices keep two histories | the pinned head, which refuses a contradicting branch after the first is accepted | the first history seen is accepted; detecting a fork nobody showed us needs witnesses |
| A device's keys are exposed | joint rotation, which the attacker cannot get anchored | rotation is not implemented here; locally pinned events are not silently rewritten, but this receipt-free profile claims no KERI accountability for them |
| A device's keys and its delegator's are compromised together | nothing at this level — only an ancestor further up the chain, through a recovery path this change does not implement | at the root there is no ancestor at all, which is what makes the root's keys worth keeping durable |
| The directory's write ticket leaks | `DeviceBindingProof` prevents full-access escalation: the holder cannot forge endpoint-bound request/manifest signatures, cannot move a complete proof to another path, and cannot promote a pending offer without the newcomer confirmation signature | the holder still reads, deletes, and writes directory material and can deny service; narrowing those capabilities is separate work |
| A device is lost or compromised beyond exposure | nothing here | no revocation and no device removal in this change, so the device keeps whatever it holds |

## Risks / Trade-offs

- [The selected di-only profile departs from the cited whitepaper] → The normative wire-profile delta fixes every event and transcript byte and includes a checked `keripy` vector; implementation tests must reproduce it before protocol code is accepted.
- [`keriox` is the complete Rust stack and is EUPL-1.2 against an Apache-2.0 workspace] → It is not linked into this Apache-2.0 workspace. Implement only the normatively fixed 3-event profile (`icp`, `dip`, and `ixn`) and its validation path over the workspace's Apache/MIT-compatible BLAKE3 and Ed25519 primitives; use pinned `keripy` only as an external test oracle. This closes the licensing choice without making library defaults part of the wire contract.
- [Losing a device freezes establishment for the devices it invited] → Named as a consequence, with re-inception under a live ancestor as the standard migration; no domain record is keyed by a device identifier — its own key events are, being the record of it — so that this stays a repair, not a data loss.
- [A device that loses its keys cannot be recovered by this runtime] → The implemented path is re-inception; section 11.6.1 still permits a future ancestor-recovery path for non-root devices, while the root has no ancestor (D3).
- [A confirmed endpoint key is lost] → Startup of the original profile fails closed. A linked device may be forgotten and linked as a new identifier from another inviter; a founder has no safe self-recovery here because forget removes root keys and root-signed replacement is the deferred recovery/supersession operation.
- [Root keys lost with the founding device foreclose rotating the root, recovering the first device, and extending the store set] → Accepted for this change, where none of the three exists; the export that would prevent it is specified by the change that needs it, so this release does not create an artefact nothing reads. The residual risk is named rather than softened: "durable" means surviving a process on the same disk, so a founding device that is lost, stolen, or destroyed takes those futures with it, and the root — unlike every other identifier in the tree — has no ancestor that could recover it.
- [An ancestor's recovery authority over its subtree is permitted by the mechanism and unimplemented here] → Left as an explicit non-goal rather than an implicit impossibility, so that a later recovery change is not read as contradicting these specs; the topology decision in front of product is stated to include it.
- [Chain verification on a read path costs more than the read] → The view is built as payload bytes arrive and the accepted head is a durable record, so ordinary reads consult a head rather than re-walking a chain; if measurement contradicts that, the question of a key-event store separate from the directory (D8) comes back, and it is cheaper to reopen than a wire format.
- [The anchor is permanent, so an abandoned attempt leaves a device identifier nobody uses] → Idempotent retry keeps one attempt from becoming several, and neither anchor nor pending offer grants access; the residue is authorization history, not a full-access binding.
- [Verification cost lands on a hot path if the chain is checked per session] → The chain is checked when the local view changes, and classification reads a cached authorization projection joining that view to confirmed records; it does not re-walk the chain per session. A record-only probe is insufficient because a directory writer can forge that half.
- [Long chains eventually outgrow any bundle limit] → The limits are explicit and refuse early, at invite time; a verifiable checkpoint replacing the full replay is the designed escape, planned before it is needed rather than after.
- [Two devices of one identity write to the same directory concurrently] → Unchanged from today for directory entries, and structurally absent for key events: each device anchors in its own log, so there is no race for a sequence number.

## Migration Plan

There is no deployed base to migrate: identities on the stand are disposable, and the protocol version and its ALPN both step to 1 with no compatibility path — a `/pdn/linking/0` dial and a version 0 payload are refused, each on its own side.

Within the workspace the order is forced by the dependencies rather than by preference: the profile decision and golden vectors first, then key storage and the identity model, then the key event layout and the validated view, then the ceremony, and the host's error table last.

The sequence beyond this change is worth stating in one place, because several decisions here defer only their general form. `durable-runtime-storage` comes before this change (D14). This change delivers inception, the chain, verification against a pinned history, the store-set seal, the bounded proof, and the immutable replicated ceremony binding required for access classification. Next comes verification of a counterparty's devices, resolution of a device identifier to a current endpoint, and supersession of a confirmed endpoint through the general endpoint statement (D9). Rotation, revocation, and recovery paths — including the ancestor authority described in D2 — follow, and they are where the root-key export (D3) and the "active chain" policy for a removed device's descendants get decided. Witnesses and receipts (D13) are last and are what would make duplicity detectable at all. A device created before this change has no key event log and cannot acquire one, so existing local state is discarded rather than upgraded.

Rollback is the same shape: nothing in this change writes to another node's state, and the anchoring events it writes are local to the identity's own devices, so abandoning the branch costs the stand's data and nothing else.

## Closed implementation choices

No protocol or product choice remains open inside this change. D2 selects the tree topology. D4 fixes all volume limits, reservation accounting, and one-deadline time budgeting. The narrow profile implementation over workspace cryptographic primitives avoids the `keriox` licensing question. Existing `PdnOp` device/key variants remain reserved and disconnected from this service until a separately versioned domain-API change defines their semantics. ADR-0003 records the identity decision; ADR-0012 remains the historical decision for version 0 until this change is implemented and archived.

The accepted view's representation is not optional: each pinned-head security record retains the complete exact-byte reconstruction set and seal index needed to re-verify it before admission, even when directory payloads have not arrived. An implementation may additionally index or cache that evidence, but it may not replace it with a head digest plus current replica arrival order.
